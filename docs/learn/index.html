Shetu in 60 seconds

When someone goes on maternity leave, her work has to pass to others and come back to her later, and today nobody manages that properly. Shetu's AI interviews her in Bangla, English or Banglish, builds an evidence-backed map of her real job, and finds the duties nobody listed. It plans who takes each duty and when, re-plans when a colleague becomes unavailable or a deadline moves, and plans her return around the arrangement she needs. AI proposes, rules check, people decide.

Lesson 1 · The problem

Nobody holds the full picture of her job.

When an employee goes on a long leave, HR records the dates and the manager improvises the rest. Click through a typical leave and see where it goes wrong.

1. Work is dropped

Duties nobody wrote down are never handed over. A deadline passes before anyone notices.

2. The plan breaks

A colleague falls ill or a deadline moves. Nobody re-plans.

3. The return does not fit

She comes back to late meetings and travel that clash with what she now needs.

Why it matters

Banks in Bangladesh had about 2,000 fewer women staff after six months (a fall of about 5%), according to Bangladesh Bank's gender report for the first half of 2025, as reported by Prothom Alo. That is the net change in the count, not a count of women who left, and it does not say why. Women are 38.4% of the labour force against 80% of men (Labour Force Survey 2024). Shetu cannot fix childcare or the law. It fixes the part a company controls: the job.

Remember

Shetu does not manage leave paperwork. It manages the job: who owns each duty, on every date, before, during and after the leave.

Lesson 2 · The big idea

Map her job. Hold her role. Fit her return.

Three stages around one shared record. Open each one.

The one rule that runs through all three
AI proposesRules checkPeople decide

The AI never approves anything and never changes a plan by itself. Code checks every proposal. A manager or HR makes the final call, and the decision is logged.

Remember

MAP understands the job. HOLD keeps it running. FIT makes the return work. One record, the Role Map, connects all three.

Lesson 3 · The hourglass

Every duty has an owner on every date.

Before leave, her duties narrow as they pass to colleagues. During leave, others hold them. On return, they widen back to her. That shape is the Hourglass. It is not extra stages: it is what the Role Map looks like when drawn over time.

Why no percentages?

An early idea had phases with fixed workload percentages. We dropped it: every person and every job is different. Her workload is simply the duties she owns on a date, and each handover and return date is agreed with her.

The coverage rule

Code checks that no duty is ever without an owner on any date. This turns "nothing is dropped" from a slogan into a test we can run and show.

Remember

Her workload is never a setting. It is the result of who owns which duty on a date. Plans are agreed individually, with no fixed percentages.

Lesson 4 · Evidence

Said, documented, suggested.

The Role Map lists her duties, and every duty shows where it came from. That is how we keep a fact apart from an AI guess.

Said

She said it. Her exact words are kept beside the duty.

Documented

A document states it. The passage is linked.

Suggested

The AI thinks it may be missing. It is only a question until she confirms it.

Rule verdict

Code passed or rejected a proposal, and shows the reason.

A real error we found. In the competition prototype she said a monthly report "can also be done from home". The AI recorded the place as "office". The quote existed in her words, so a quote check passed, but the quote did not support that field. That is why our test measures evidence accuracy: every filled field must be supported, not just the quote exist.
Remember

A suggestion never becomes a fact on its own. She confirms it, or it stays a question.

Lesson 5 · Who does what

AI proposes. Rules check. People decide.

Shetu is AI-native because without the AI it collapses into a form that records only what people remember. But the AI is deliberately not trusted with everything. Sort the jobs.

AI

Understanding mixed Bangla and English, noticing what is missing, drafting plans. Things rules cannot do.

Rules

Dates, times, numbers, workload, permissions, the coverage rule, the audit log. Things that must always be right.

People

Confirming her map, approving plans, deciding exceptions. Everything final.

The "remove the AI" test

If we deleted all AI, the interview, the forgotten-duty questions, the document matching and "Tania is away three weeks" would stop working. What remains is a leave form with an approval flow. That is the manual process we are replacing, so the AI is the core, not a decoration.

Remember

Anything that needs judgement about language goes to the AI. Anything that must be exactly right goes to code. Anything final goes to a person.

Lesson 6 · Hold

When the plan breaks, Shetu re-plans.

This is the most AI-native part and the one we want to show working by the 60% update. A manager types what changed. Step through what happens.

Things to notice
  • The AI asks when a fact is missing (the start date). It does not guess.
  • Dates are computed by code, from the facts the AI extracted.
  • A proposal can be rejected by the rules and shown with the reason.
  • A person approves. Nothing changes before that.
Remember

The AI reads and proposes. Code finds the impact and checks capacity. The manager approves. Every step is logged.

Lesson 7 · How the app is built

React, FastAPI, SQLite: simple to explain.

Two halves that talk through one API. Click each part, then play a request through it.

Why React + FastAPI
  • Our instructor recommended it.
  • Frontend and backend can be built in parallel by different people.
  • FastAPI is Python, so the AI code plugs in directly, and it is built on Pydantic, the same tool that validates AI output.
What we are not using

Microservices, vector databases, agent frameworks, LangChain, Redux, Next.js. They add complexity we do not need, and we must be able to explain every part.

Lesson 8 · The AI gateway

Talking to the AI safely.

The app never calls a model directly. It calls one function, our gateway, which checks every reply. Try breaking it.

Why a gateway
  • Change the model without rewriting the app.
  • One place that validates, retries, falls back and logs.
  • A replay mode returns recorded answers, so tests are predictable and free.
For a real company

A hosted AI sends text to a third party. A real company may refuse that for HR data, so the same app can run on a local model inside their own servers. For this lab we use a hosted model with fake data only.

Remember

A model reply is a suggestion in a fixed shape. If it does not fit the shape, it is rejected, retried once, then handed to the next provider. A wrong reply never reaches the database.

Deeper 1 · Rules, exactly

What a rule actually is.

When we say "rules check", we mean ordinary Python code: plain arithmetic and if/else logic. No AI, no guessing. The same input always gives the same answer, and every rule has tests. They will live in backend/app/rules/ (planned, not built).

Why rules exist

An AI model can be confidently wrong about numbers and dates. So the model never decides anything a program can calculate. The AI proposes; only the rules can say a proposal is valid; a person still decides.

The rules Shetu will have

RuleWhat it doesExample
DatesComputes dates and ranges from the facts the AI pulled out of a sentence"Three weeks from the start date" becomes an exact end date
ImpactFinds which duties a change affectsTania is away, so the renewals and the quarterly report are hit
WorkloadChecks a colleague has enough spare hoursRafiq has 2 spare hours, the job needs 3: rejected. Imran has 3 of 4: passed
CoverageNo duty may be without an owner on any dateAny gap in the ownership timeline fails the plan
PolicyChecks a proposal against company policy (one policy rule in the first version, for FIT)A return arrangement must follow the company's rule
Time clashesFinds meetings or travel that clash with the arrangement she asked for (FIT)A 7:30 pm meeting against an agreed finish time
Evidence"Said" or "documented" needs a matching quote or document; "suggested" stays a question until a person confirmsThe quarter-end review stays a suggestion until she says yes
Output shapeEvery AI reply must match a fixed format, or it is retried once and then rejectedA wrong reply never reaches the database

What a rule looks like in code

# a rule is a normal function: facts in, a verdict out
def has_capacity(free_hours, needed_hours):
    return free_hours >= needed_hours

has_capacity(2, 3)   # Rafiq  False
has_capacity(3, 3)   # Imran  True
If you are asked

"A rule is plain code that checks something we can calculate, like dates, workload or whether a duty has an owner. The AI can propose, but only the rules can say a proposal is valid, and a person still decides."

Remember

"Rules" is not a fancy engine or machine learning. It is normal Python functions with tests. Nothing is built yet, so say "the rules will check".

Deeper 2 · Tests and pytest

"Tested with pytest", in plain words.

A test is a small piece of code that runs your real code and checks the answer is right. pytest is the Python tool that finds all the tests, runs them, and tells you which passed and which failed.

def test_rafiq_has_too_little_time():
    # Rafiq has 2 free hours, the job needs 3
    assert has_capacity(free_hours=2, needed_hours=3) == False

def test_imran_has_enough_time():
    assert has_capacity(free_hours=3, needed_hours=3) == True

assert means "this must be true". If someone later changes the rule by accident and Rafiq suddenly passes, this test fails and tells us before a manager ever sees a wrong plan.

pytest: the inside

Tests the backend code: the rules, the API routes, the AI gateway. Fast, runs in seconds.

Selenium: the outside

Opens a real browser and clicks through the app like a user would: log in, type a change, press Approve.

Two more words
  • Replay adapter. In tests, the AI gateway plays back recorded AI answers instead of calling a real model. So tests are free, fast, and give the same result every time.
  • CI (continuous integration). GitHub runs all the tests automatically on every pull request, so broken code cannot be merged without anyone noticing.
Remember

The rules are the safety net around the AI. Tests are how we know the net has no holes.

Deeper 3 · The architecture, piece by piece

Shetu as a restaurant.

Every technical part, what it is, and why it is there. All of this is the plan; nothing is built yet.

RestaurantShetu
Dining room and menuFrontend (React): what people see and click
WaiterAPI: carries requests and answers between the screen and the kitchen
KitchenBackend (FastAPI): does the real work
Head chef who checks every plateRules: plain code that validates
A specialist chef who reads and writesThe AI model
One supplier contract, with a spare supplierAI gateway: one door to whichever model we use
Pantry and recordsDatabase (SQLite)

Each part

1 · Frontend: React (with Vite)

Everything a person sees and clicks: the interview chat, the Role Map, the ownership timeline, the Approve button. It runs in the browser. React builds screens from small reusable pieces called components (a duty row, a button) and redraws only what changed. Vite is the tool that runs and builds the React project quickly. The frontend holds no business logic: it shows data and sends what the user did.

2 · The API

The agreed set of requests the frontend may send, such as "give me the Role Map for case 7". The backend answers in JSON, plain text like {"duty": "Monthly report", "owner": "Tania"}. The browser sends requests with fetch. Because the two halves only share this agreed format, one person can build screens while another builds the backend.

3 · Backend: Python and FastAPI

Runs on a server and holds everything important: login, roles, the case workflow, the rules, the AI calls and the database. FastAPI receives each request and sends it to the right Python function. Our instructor recommended it, and Python is the language of AI work. Permissions are checked here, on every route: hiding a button is not security, because anyone can send a request without the button.

4 · Pydantic: a form every message must fill

Describes the exact shape data must have: a duty has a title (text), an owner (a known person), hours (a number). Anything that does not match is rejected. We use it on API requests and, most importantly, on AI replies.

5 · Database: SQLite and SQLAlchemy

A database stores data permanently in tables, like spreadsheets: cases, duties, owners over time, proposals, approvals, the audit log. SQLite is a database in a single file with nothing to install, so it is the same on every laptop. SQLAlchemy lets Python treat tables as objects, so we write case.duties instead of database commands.

6 · Rules

Plain Python for dates, impact, workload, policy, coverage and evidence. See Deeper 1.

7 · The AI gateway

The app never calls a model directly. Every request goes through our gateway, which builds the request from our prompt (the written instructions for the model), sends it to the configured model, checks the reply against the Pydantic shape, retries once, falls back if it still fails, and logs what happened. Behind it can sit a hosted model (an AI company's server), a local model (an open model such as Qwen3-4B on the company's own machine, so data never leaves), or the replay adapter for tests. Changing the model means changing one setting.

8 · Tests and 9 · GitHub

pytest for the backend, Selenium for user flows (Deeper 2). One shared repository; every feature goes Issue, branch, commits, pull request, review by another member, merge. The history shows who built what.

One request, start to finish

  1. The manager types "Tania will be away for three weeks, and the report is now due April 2" in a React screen.
  2. React sends it to the API.
  3. FastAPI checks the manager is allowed to do this.
  4. The backend calls the AI gateway, which sends the text to the model.
  5. The model returns facts (who, how long, the new date). The start date is missing, so it asks.
  6. Pydantic checks the reply has the right shape.
  7. Rules compute the dates and the affected duties, and check workload and coverage.
  8. The AI proposes Rafiq; the rules reject him (2 free hours, 3 needed). The next proposal passes.
  9. The proposal is saved as "waiting for approval" and written to the audit log.
  10. React shows it; the manager approves or changes it.
Remember

Screens in React, logic in FastAPI, data in SQLite. The AI is reached only through our gateway, so it is replaceable and its answers are checked. Dates and numbers are never left to the model.

Deeper 4 · The AI pipeline

The model is one step of nine.

An AI pipeline is the fixed sequence of steps that turns what a person types into a checked, usable result. "The AI pipeline" in our backend means everything that happens around the model, not only the model.

#StepWhat happensWho
1InputA person's words arrive: an interview answer, or "Tania will be away for three weeks"Code
2Build the promptWrap the text in our instructions: what to extract, the answer format, plus context such as her job descriptionCode
3Gateway sends itTo whichever model is configured: hosted, local, or replay in testsCode
4The model answersReads Banglish or English and returns facts or a proposalAI
5ValidatePydantic checks the exact shape; retry once, then fall back or report failureCode
6Check the evidenceEvery duty must point to her exact words or a document; otherwise it is only "suggested"Code
7Rules checkDates, affected duties, workload, policy, coverage; pass or reject with a reasonCode
8Save and logStored as "waiting for approval" and written to the audit logCode
9A person decidesManager or HR sees what the AI understood and the proposal, then approves or changes itPeople

One pipeline per stage

MAP

The AI chooses the next interview question, extracts duties with her words as evidence, matches them against her job description, finds duties she never mentioned (as questions), and drafts who takes each one.

HOLD

The manager types a change. The AI turns it into facts and asks if one is missing. Code computes dates and impact, the AI proposes a fix, the rules check it, the manager approves.

FIT

She states the arrangement she needs. Rules find the time clashes. The AI proposes fixes. Rules check them, and people decide.

Why a pipeline, not "just ask the model"
  • Wrong answers are caught by validation, evidence checks and rules, before anyone sees them as fact.
  • The model is replaceable, because the pipeline is the same for any model.
  • Tests are repeatable: with replay, steps 5 to 8 are tested without calling a real model.
  • It is auditable: every step is logged.
Remember

"We build the prompt, our gateway sends it, we validate and evidence-check the reply, the rules verify it, and a person approves. The model is one step; the rest is ordinary code we test."

Deeper 5 · Tokens and embeddings

Tokenization and vectorization.

Our instructor mentioned these after the proposal presentation. Here is what they mean and where they could fit Shetu.

Tokenization

AI models do not read words; they read tokens, small pieces of text. "Shetu maps duties" is about four or five tokens. Cost, speed and how much text fits in one request are all measured in tokens.

Why it matters for us: Bangla usually needs many more tokens than English for the same meaning, and Banglish splits differently again. So Bangla input costs more, is slower, and fills the model's memory faster in long interviews. We can count tokens per test case in the model bake-off, and keep prompts short.

Vectorization (embeddings)

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a list of numbers, a vector, so that texts with similar meaning get similar numbers. "Credit meeting every Tuesday" and "weekly credit committee" share almost no words, but their vectors are close. A computer can then compare meaning, not just spelling, using a measure called cosine similarity.

# the idea, not real numbers
"credit meeting every Tuesday"  -> [0.12, -0.40, 0.88, ...]
"weekly credit committee"       -> [0.10, -0.38, 0.85, ...]   # close: same meaning
"submit the monthly report"     -> [-0.70, 0.22, 0.05, ...]   # far: different meaning

Where embeddings could fit Shetu

  • MAP gap finding: match what she said against each job-description line, so duties she never mentioned stand out. That is our "forgotten duty" feature.
  • Duplicates: "monthly report" and "the report I send each month" become one duty.
  • FIT: find the policy paragraph that relates to her return request.
  • Never to match or rank colleagues. That would break the rule "never score people".
How, without breaking our simple design
  • No vector database is needed: a few hundred lines fit in SQLite, and Python compares the vectors directly.
  • The embedding call goes through the same AI gateway.
  • Embeddings shortlist possible matches; the language model decides with evidence; rules and a person confirm.
  • We test it on our fake cases with and without embeddings, and keep it only if it finds more missed duties.
Agreed on 23 November 2026. The team accepted the plan to test embeddings first: we may use them, we will try them on the same fake cases, and we keep them only if they help. The decision is in docs/DECISIONS.md.
Remember

Tokens are how a model counts text. Embeddings turn meaning into numbers so we can compare it. One line for the instructor: "We may use embeddings to find forgotten duties by meaning. We will test on the same fake cases whether they help before we decide."

Deeper 6 · Our niche

"Why only mothers? Isn't it a work planner?"

We were asked this in both places we presented on 15 November. It means the pitch had a gap, not the product. Here is how a product manager answers it.

Agreed on 23 November 2026. The team accepted this framing as our answer. The decision is in docs/DECISIONS.md.
First, agree

The engine is general: find the duties in a job, keep an owner for every duty on every date, re-plan when things change. That is a strength. Successful products start narrow, with the one group whose pain is sharpest (a beachhead market), then expand.

Why maternity first

ReasonWhy it matters
You can see it comingKnown months ahead, so there is time for MAP. Sick leave gives no warning
It is longMonths, not days: long enough that work really is dropped and the job drifts
She comes back differentHours, travel and late meetings may no longer fit. That is FIT, and no planner has a return stage
It is where companies lose peopleAbout 2,000 fewer women in Bangladesh's banks after six months. The cost is a trained person, not a missed task
It is sensitiveYou must not ask why, or store medical or family details. Generic tools are not built with those limits
There is a clear buyerHR has a budget and a target: keeping women after they return

Why it is not a work planner

Work planner (Asana, Jira, Trello)HR leave systemShetu
Knows the workOnly tasks someone already typedNo; only leave datesFinds the job, including unwritten duties, from her words and documents, with evidence
OwnershipWho has a task nowNoWho owns every duty on every date, with the coverage rule

The returnNo such ideaRecords the datePlans the job to fit her life
The answer, out loud

"A planner manages work you already know about. Shetu finds the work nobody wrote down, and keeps a person's job whole while she is away and when she comes back. We start with maternity because it is the long leave you can see coming, and the return is where companies lose people. The same engine then handles any long leave."

Where it grows

  1. Now: maternity leave, in Bangladeshi banks and large employers with many skilled women.
  2. Next: any planned long leave: paternity, planned surgery, study leave, Hajj, a sabbatical. MAP, HOLD and FIT still apply.
  3. Later: unplanned absence, and people who resign, so their unwritten knowledge is captured before they go.

What an earlier build of ours already fixed

Before the lab build, our team made a separate public version of Shetu for the AI Builders Congress 2026. Its history shows what we fixed after being asked the same sort of questions. We carry the ideas over, not the code.

  • Gender-neutral wording. Screens, messages, prompts and rule texts say "the employee" or "they", so every planned leave reads correctly. Maternity is our design focus, never a data field.
  • Any planned leave of two weeks or more. Study leave runs through the same flow with different dates and duties.
  • Never how a pregnancy becomes known. There is no field for the reason, so Shetu cannot reveal it.
  • Temporary means temporary. Every assignment ends, and nothing becomes permanent without the employee's agreement. A work planner does not guard this.
  • Compare against a plain checklist and a general chatbot on the same fake cases, to show what Shetu adds, plus interviews with managers and HR. Both are planned for the lab, not done.
The business, if they push
  • Customer: banks, NGOs, telecoms and IT companies with many women in skilled roles.
  • Who pays: HR. Who uses it: the employee, her manager, the colleagues who cover.
  • Pricing idea: per active leave case, or per employee per year, after a free pilot.
  • How success is measured: share of women who return, still employed 12 months later, zero dropped duties, manager time spent re-planning.
  • Biggest risks: managers not using it, and trust in the AI. Evidence on every duty and manager approval are our answer to both.
Remember

Do not add features to answer this. Change the explanation, and make the demo show what a planner cannot: the forgotten duty in MAP, the plain-language re-plan in HOLD, and the return fit. "Built for maternity, ready for any long leave."
